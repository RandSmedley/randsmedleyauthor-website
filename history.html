The history behind the story

Best read after you’ve finished The Geronimo Solution. A few of these notes give away what really happened in Tombstone.

How it began

The year 2020 was a hard one for most of us on planet Earth. The COVID-19 pandemic hit some communities harder than others, but all of us had to make adjustments to how we lived our lives. As a flight instructor, I found it impossible to maintain “social distance” in the cockpit of a small airplane, so I decided to retire.

To keep myself occupied, I bought a PlayStation 5 and began playing video games. Two of my favorites were Red Dead Redemption 2, an open-world, action-adventure game set in the last days of the American Old West, and EVE Online, a space-based multiplayer role-playing game online.

Even playing sophisticated video games, I became bored. I began wondering what would happen if Arthur Morgan, the outlaw protagonist of Red Dead Redemption 2, found himself aboard one of EVE Online’s military starships. Wouldn’t that be cool? How would he react? Could he adapt?

From these musings, The Geronimo Solution was conceived. The gestation to a completed manuscript took three years.

Relevant historical facts

Regarding the prologue, Space Shuttle Discovery flew shuttle mission STS-51C in January of 1985, crewed by Mission Commander Thomas K. (Ken) Mattingly, Pilot Loren J. Shriver, Mission Specialists Ellison S. Onizuka and James F. Buchli, and Payload Specialist Gary E. Payton. It was the fifteenth flight of the space shuttle program, the third flight for Discovery, and the first mission flown exclusively for the Department of Defense, delivering a classified payload into orbit. The mission itself was shrouded in secrecy, with very little coverage of the flight offered by NASA.

Sadly, Ellison Onizuka lost his life a year later in the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster that cost the lives of six others.

The Tombstone fire in 1881 that Harry Hennessey accidentally started when the ash from his cigar fell into a barrel of whiskey actually happened! A barrel of whiskey had been moved to the boardwalk in front of the Arcade Saloon with the plug removed from the bung hole. A bartender’s cigar ash fell into the opening, igniting the contents, which quickly got out of control. Strong desert winds pushed the flames through town.

And yes, as Harry Hennessey said, a year later another fire, worse than the first, hit Tombstone and nearly destroyed it.

Ted Wellish, proprietor of a Tucson dry goods store, indeed owned the whitewashed adobe cabin where protagonist John McAllister lived. The cabin and Vanguard’s Ambush Mine were both located in White House Canyon, as the canyon was called back in the 1880s. Known today as Madera Canyon, it cuts southeast into the Santa Rita Mountains, a “sky island” mountain range about forty miles south of Tucson. And as mentioned in the novel, a small molybdenum mine was started in the canyon back in the 1930s just a mile or so south of the cabin.

Attracting birdwatchers from around the world, today, Madera Canyon is a beautiful National Forest Recreational Area with hiking, camping, and picnic facilities. The remains of Wellish’s cabin are a short walk from the White House Picnic Area.

Tombstone’s silver mines did hit a major snag when the trickle of water seeping into the lower levels became a torrent. Two of the mines, the Grand Central and the Contention, each installed huge Cornish steam engines to drive pumps to drain their claims. They also drained all the claims in the area and, as relayed by Ezra in the story, the other mine owners gleefully refused to help defray any of the expenses for the pumping operations. Many hard feelings ensued.

On the night of May 24, 1886, according to the diary of George Parsons, a Tombstone resident, a wildfire began at the base of the Dragoon Mountains north of the town. Because of Tombstone’s susceptibility to fire, the residents stayed up to keep an eye on the fire and to fend off any stray sparks that might find purchase in town. Did Geronimo set this fire? Well, probably not in our timeline.

Two days later, on the night of May 26, a fire started in the surface works at Grand Central Mine. It became so hot that it melted the Cornish steam engine, destroying it. To this day, nobody knows how the fire started or how it could have gotten that hot. Of course, readers will know it was a wayward plasma round that did all the damage.

The remaining engine at the Contention Mine was not enough to keep the water from rising hundreds of feet. Before another engine could be ordered and shipped, the bottom fell out of the silver market. Tombstone’s boom had busted, and the lower levels were abandoned. Tombstone never fully recovered.

It is also true that a couple of the Tombstone silver mines were pulling up wulfenite, a soft, tabular crystal that, at the time, had no value. Today, it is a valuable mineral from which molybdenum and lead are extracted. In 2017, wulfenite was named the Arizona state mineral.

The real people

Regarding the characters in the story, a number of them, such as Theodore Wellish, are historical figures, real people who lived real lives. Below is a list of other characters I lifted from history. Some are recognizable to anyone who grew up watching Hollywood westerns or has even a slight interest in the American Old West. Others are obscure to all but true students of that time and place.

I inserted many of these real people into this alternate timeline and stayed as true to their known physical and personality traits as I could. It was a joy to bring all of these people from history back to life and introduce them to the readers of an admittedly fanciful tale.

Sheriff Robert Paul

Born in Lowell, Massachusetts, 1830; died 1901 in Tucson, Arizona. Bob stood 6 feet, 6 inches and weighed over 240 pounds, making him one of the largest men in the territory. After a contested election, he served as sheriff of Pima County from early 1881 through 1886. Shortly after he took office, the eastern half of Pima County was split off to form Cochise County. Johnny Behan, a disreputable man, became sheriff of Cochise County, and Tombstone became the county seat. Robert Paul lived an interesting life and, by all accounts, was an honorable lawman, one of the few who didn’t have a shady past.

Geronimo (Goyaałé or Goyahkla, “One Who Yawns”)

Born 1829; died 1909. Much has been written about Geronimo, a medicine man and military leader of the Chiricahua band of Apache Indians. Geronimo himself dictated and published his autobiography. He was a fierce warrior with a love for whiskey and a hatred for Mexicans.

Geronimo was reported to have the mystical ability to see the future or know events happening more than fifty miles away. I have tried to treat all of the Apaches, and Geronimo in particular, with respect, although I didn’t want to minimize his brutal methods of warfare against both the Mexicans and the Americans. War is a brutal business, and Geronimo was good at it.

Naiche (also Natchez)

Born 1857; died 1919. Naiche was the youngest son of Cochise and the final hereditary chief of the Chiricahua Apaches. Naiche was never meant to be chief. His brother Taza became chief when Cochise died in 1874. Two years later, Taza died, so the office fell to nineteen-year-old Naiche, who often deferred to Geronimo.

Yanozha

Born 1865; died 1954. Pictured with Geronimo and Chappo in a famous March 1886 photograph by C.S. Fly, Yanozha was Geronimo’s brother-in-law.

Chappo Geronimo

The oldest son of Geronimo, most records say he was born in 1867. After being captured with his father and Naiche, he went to Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania where, by all accounts, he did well. Unfortunately, he caught tuberculosis and was sent home. He died in 1894.

Lulu Geronimo (Dohn-Zay)

Born 1865; died 1898. Daughter of Geronimo, older sister to Chappo, and mother of Nina.

Zohnne and Mohtsos

Both were among the younger braves captured when Geronimo’s band surrendered to General Miles in September of 1886. Carlisle Indian School has a record and photo of Zohnne.

Dr. N. Matas

A physician listed in the 1881 Tucson Directory; located at 227 S. Main St. His son, Rudolph Matas, followed his father into medicine and became a renowned surgical pioneer.

Dr. George E. Goodfellow

Born December 23, 1855; died December 7, 1910. Worthy of his own book, George Goodfellow is considered the first true trauma surgeon in the United States, pioneering the use of sterile instruments and hands. He started a practice in Tombstone, Arizona, where he saved many victims of gunshot wounds, including Virgil Earp. He also performed the first recorded successful removal of an enlarged prostate and pioneered facial reconstructive surgery. The previously mentioned diarist, George Parsons, suffered a terrible injury in the first Tombstone fire when a burning balcony fell on him, severely crushing his nose. According to Parsons’s diary, Dr. Goodfellow built a scaffold of copper wire to support the soft tissues until the cartilage could heal. Parsons reported he was pleased with the results, and photos of him taken years later show no noticeable sign of the injury.

Sheriff Robert Hatch

Born 1841; died 1904. Robert Hatch, shortly after arriving in Tombstone, was a part owner of a saloon there. He witnessed the shootout at the OK Corral and the fatal shooting of Morgan Earp not long after. Hatch served as deputy for a while. He was made sheriff of Cochise County and was based in Tombstone in 1885 and 1886.

John P. Clum

Born September 1, 1851; died May 2, 1932. Although not a character in the novel, Mac tells a story that includes the mayor of Tombstone, John Clum.

Before he moved to Tombstone, John Clum had been an Indian Agent who managed the San Carlos (Apache) Indian Reservation. He and one hundred Indian police once captured Geronimo without having to fire a shot. All reports of him in his dealings with the Apaches found him to be fair-minded, which put him at odds with both the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the US Army. Both often profited by withholding supplies meant for the reservation. Even Geronimo thought well of Clum.

Clum quit as Indian agent for the BIA, moved to Tombstone, and in May of 1880, published the first edition of The Tombstone Epitaph. A big proponent of law and order, he was soon elected Tombstone’s first mayor. He appointed Virgil Earp town marshal and became close friends with Wyatt Earp. In December of 1880, Clum’s wife, Mary, died shortly after giving birth to their daughter. The following summer, in 1881, the sickly child also died.

After the gunfight at the OK Corral in October of that year, the outlaw element known as the Cochise County Cowboys started gunning for the Earps and their friends, including Clum. After Virgil Earp was wounded and Morgan Earp killed, Clum left Tombstone behind, while the Earps began their infamous vendetta ride.

Clum, Wyatt Earp, and George Parsons met up again years later in Nome, Alaska, where John Clum had been made a postal inspector.
